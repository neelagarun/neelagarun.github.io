I'm Nikhil Neelagaru. I did my undergrad at Boston College, where I majored in biology but
spent most of my time in an experimental condensed matter physics lab. We built nanoscale coaxial electrode arrays,
grew stem cells around them, and passed current through the solution to see how it changed the way the cells
differentiated, as part of research on neural interfaces. A lot of my work there was running the scanning electron
microscopes and writing Python to measure the structures in the images, and I later worked out the physics of why
those electrodes keep their fields so local in [this post](/physics/2026/07/05/nanocoax-field-locality.html). I also
founded the Bioengineering Society at BC, which grew from five members to over a hundred, and worked part-time as a
machine learning engineering intern at Aventure Trading, building portfolio models in Python and TensorFlow.

After BC I spent a stretch in New York as the lead chess instructor at Success Academy, which runs the top-ranked
public school chess program in the state. I ran the K-5 after-school program and coached teams for city, state, and
national tournaments.

I then went to Boston University for a master's in Mathematical Finance and Financial Technology, which I finished in
January 2026. While I was there I did computational physics research in a statistical mechanics group, working on
non-equilibrium systems with stochastic processes, mean-field theory, ergodic theory, and network science, and
building Python simulations to test our ideas. Some of that work had economic implications, especially for the
so-called Yard-Sale model of wealth exchange, which I've kept thinking about [here](/statistics/physics/2026/10/07/yard-sale-common-noise.html).

Now I'm a co-founder of Correllia Research, a quantitative research firm that builds machine learning and
statistical models for financial firms without in-house quant desks. I work on the engineering and model development, which includes our credit-risk and macro forecasting models and Quanta, the desktop platform our clients use to run those
models on their own data. The part of the work I like most is taking a model that holds up statistically and turning
it into a system other people can rely on, and a couple of the posts here, on
[credit risk](/machine-learning/statistics/2026/09/19/credit-risk-decision-problem.html) and
[CPI forecasting](/machine-learning/2026/04/21/forecasting-cpi.html), come straight out of that.

Outside of work, tennis is probably the thing I love most. I'm always looking for a match when I can find the time,
and my favorite player has always been Rafael Nadal. It has a way of creeping into everything else I do, so I've spent
time applying machine learning and statistics to predictive analytics in sports, and I worked through the physics of
why a hard first serve only has about a degree of room to land in [this post](/physics/2026/07/24/tennis-serve-geometry.html).

I also love spending time with my friends, and a lot of that time goes into traveling somewhere new together. We've camped in the queue at Wimbledon, gone boating around Denmark, and visited Istanbul, and between trips like those I've made it to 23 countries so far. I'd like to get to all of them eventually, and I worked out roughly how long that might take [here](/travel/2026/06/16/countries-coupon-collector.html).
